Section 1The problem: a free vaccine, a hesitant parent

Cervical cancer is preventable, yet Kazakhstan’s first HPV vaccine programme collapsed under parental refusal. The vaccine is now back.

Read the full analysis

Cervical cancer is one of the most preventable cancers, yet it is the second most common cancer among women in Kazakhstan (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). In 2021 it had a crude incidence of 18.3 cases per 100,000 women; the same paper puts the burden at about 1,800 new cases and 600 deaths a year (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). Almost all of it is caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV), and a vaccine given before adolescence prevents most of it. The World Health Organization's goal is for 90% of girls to be fully vaccinated by age 15 by 2030 (WHO, 2020).

Kazakhstan has tried this before. Its first HPV vaccination pilot collapsed after negative media reports about side effects were followed by mass parental refusal (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). The sources do not even agree on its dates (see the timeline). Halting the programme also cost Kazakhstan its place in the global vaccine supply queue (Astana Times, 2025).

Since 2024 the vaccine is free again, but each year first doses still reach less than half of the target group.

Read the full analysis

Since September 2024 the vaccine has been free again, offered to girls aged 11–13 and, since May 2026, to girls aged 14–17 (Ministry of Healthcare, 2026), and for girls aged 11–13 it is delivered in schools after a parent signs consent (Kassabekova et al., 2025). Figure 1 shows how far the programme has come and how far it still has to go.

First-dose coverage rose from 38% to 47% in the programme's first full year, but the number of first doses given in 2025 still equalled less than half of the target group. Low HPV coverage is common worldwide: WHO estimates global first-dose coverage among girls at 33% in 2025 (WHO, 2026b).

Who is affected, and who decides

Girls are at risk, but parents, usually mothers, decide by signing a voluntary consent form.

Read the full analysis

The people at risk are girls, but the people who decide are their parents. Vaccination is voluntary and requires a parent's signature (Ministry of Healthcare, 2026), and in practice mothers play a significant role in that decision, which is why Kazakh survey research on the vaccine focuses on them (Babi et al., 2023). So the unit of this problem is a single household decision, made quickly, often in a messenger chat, about a vaccine linked to sex and cancer and to a programme that failed once before.

My social-science questionKazakhstan gave up on this vaccine once, after negative media reports turned into mass parental refusal. Whether the relaunch ends differently depends on one question: at the moment a parent decides, whom do they trust, and who could earn that trust back?

Section 2Background & data: what is counted, and what is not

Three official sources count HPV vaccination: coverage reports, Ministry totals and a refusal register. They do not fully agree.

Read the full analysis

Three official sources count HPV vaccination in Kazakhstan. Administrative coverage is reported every year by the Ministry to WHO, which publishes its own estimates alongside it. Running totals appear in Ministry press posts. A national refusal register records parents who formally decline a vaccine, and started recording HPV in 2025 (Chayakova et al., 2026, preprint). What people think about the vaccine comes from surveys and focus groups, which are reviewed in Section 3. Each official source answers a different question, and the gaps between them matter as much as the numbers.

Same doses, two different percentages

The same 90,613 doses give 47% or 43.91%, depending on who counts the target group.

Read the full analysis

The clearest example comes from the official coverage data. For 2025, Kazakhstan's own report and the WHO estimate count exactly the same 90,613 first doses. But they divide them by different target populations, and so they arrive at different coverage rates.

A three-point gap may look minor, but it means that nobody can say with certainty how many girls the programme is supposed to reach. Every target, budget and evaluation, including one for an AI tool, rests on that denominator.

A register that sometimes counts backwards

In its first year recording HPV, the register counted 9,209 refusals, almost two-thirds in Aktobe and Zhambyl. The register itself has errors.

Read the full analysis

The refusal register should be the most direct measure of hesitancy. According to a new preprint that has not yet been peer-reviewed, registered refusals of all vaccines rose from 39,792 to 66,240 between 2024 and 2026. In its first year recording HPV, the register counted 9,209 HPV refusals, 13.9% of all refusers, with almost two-thirds in Aktobe (3,037) and Zhambyl (2,791) (Chayakova et al., 2026, preprint). The same study, however, finds that the register records 3.35–3.84 times more BCG refusals than routine coverage reports do, and that in 46 of 180 region–vaccine pairs a cumulative count went down from one year to the next, which a cumulative count should never do. Across all vaccines, personal beliefs were the leading reason (62.1%), followed by religion (15.5%). The register does not publish reasons for refusing HPV separately; for those, the focus groups with mothers of girls are the main source (Kassabekova et al., 2025).

Taken together, the data is good enough to say that coverage is far below target and that refusal is concentrated geographically. It is not good enough to say precisely how many girls are missed, who their parents are, or what would change their minds. Any honest proposal has to be designed for that uncertainty, and has to measure its own effect rather than assume it.

Section 3Current state of research

Two approaches: what parents think about HPV vaccination, and which interventions raise it.

Read the full analysis

Research on HPV vaccination in Kazakhstan is recent and small, and several of the key studies come from one research group at Nazarbayev University. I review it in two approaches. The first is studies of attitudes: surveys and focus groups that ask women and mothers what they know, believe and trust. The second is studies of interventions: what has been tried around the world to raise HPV vaccination.

Three Kazakh studies ask, in different ways, what people think about the HPV vaccine. They use different methods and different samples, and they do not produce a single answer.

The comparison is revealing. Two surveys in the same cities, in the same months, find 54% and 45% positive, and the figure drops to 41% among the people who actually decide: mothers of daughters. Part of the gap is likely about whom they sampled: the first survey also included women without children. In these data, the more personally the decision concerns people, the less positive they appear. The focus groups explain the numbers rather than repeat them: they show that hesitation is built on specific beliefs, such as fear of infertility and the belief that cervical cancer is hereditary. All three studies point the same way, but none of them measures attitudes after the 2024 relaunch, and none of the parent studies is representative of rural families.

1. What mothers know and believe

Mothers know little, the undecided know least, and they trust doctors far more than the state.

Read the full analysis

Two surveys conducted between December 2021 and February 2022 in four large cities give the clearest picture. Among 191 mothers, only 45% had a positive attitude to HPV vaccination; among mothers of daughters, the group that actually decides, the figure was 41% (Babi et al., 2023). Knowledge was strikingly low, a mean of 2.4 correct answers out of 12, and the most important finding is who knew least: mothers with neutral attitudes, whose median score was zero.

Where do mothers get their information? Almost 40% from social media and TV, and 35% from gynaecologists and family doctors; schools and paediatricians were among the least-used sources, and of mothers who reached a decision, only 36% found it easy (Babi et al., 2023). That matters because the programme itself is delivered through schools (Kassabekova et al., 2025). Stigma also appears directly in the data: among mothers who agreed it was hard to talk to their daughters about sexual health, 46% had negative attitudes to the vaccine.

The second survey, of 233 women, found 54% positive, and asked a question that goes to the heart of this project: whom do they trust?

Women trust scientists and medical workers several times more than the government, and the least trusted source is the messenger apps where rumours spread. Lower education, more children, belief in alternative medicine and having been affected by the failed 2013 programme were all linked to less positive attitudes (Aimagambetova et al., 2022). Focus groups in five cities add the human detail. Mothers had little awareness of HPV and its vaccine, many believed cervical cancer to be hereditary, and fears about infertility, safety and efficacy fuelled strong opposition. They decided after talking to family, doctors and peers, and trusted doctors only when they seemed competent and explained clearly. Most preferred vaccination in clinics; parents in Almaty and Taldykorgan were open to vaccination at school, while those in Pavlodar, Uralsk and Karaganda opposed it (Kassabekova et al., 2025, Methods 2.1.1 and section 3.4.2).

2. What works: communication, trust and the doctor’s voice

Three lessons from abroad: communicate early with clear safety messages and a quick answer to rumours, expect a scare to cost years even when it is reversed, and let doctors recommend the vaccine as routine. Information alone is not enough.

The evidence on how to raise HPV vaccination is large but lopsided. A systematic review of 79 intervention studies found that 72.2% came from the United States, the largest group (31.6%) only gave information, and 76.3% worked at a single level, such as parents only or clinics only. Its conclusion: education alone is not enough, and programmes need to work at several levels at once (Escoffery et al., 2023). Three studies show what those levels look like in practice.

1. Communicate early, and answer rumours fast. A review of HPV vaccination in 45 low- and middle-income countries puts communication at the centre. Social mobilisation should start at least a month before vaccination. Messages should cover cancer prevention, safety and efficacy (including the lack of any effect on fertility), government endorsement and the second dose. Members of government or WHO representatives should respond to rumours as quickly as possible, and consent processes should match those of routine vaccines, to avoid rumours (Gallagher et al., 2017, Table 5). For Kazakhstan, the weak point is the voice this advice relies on: the government is the source women trust least.

2. A scare can be reversed, but slowly and at a cost. In Denmark, extensive negative media coverage cut uptake to about half of its earlier level: 75% of girls born in 1999 and 2000 started vaccination at age 12, but only 25% of those born in 2004. Early responses relied on dry scientific information that did not engage the public. What followed was a campaign run by a coalition of the Danish Health Agency, the Danish Cancer Society and the Danish Medical Association, aimed at the 34% of parents who were hesitant rather than the 14% who were unwilling, through town-hall meetings with health professionals and parent-to-parent messages (“My daughter is HPV vaccinated because…”). Uptake recovered, but an estimated 26,000 fewer girls started vaccination (Hansen et al., 2020, Tables 1 and 2).

3. How doctors speak matters. In a randomised trial in 30 clinics in North Carolina, clinics whose doctors were trained to give brief “announcements”, which assume parents are ready to vaccinate, raised HPV vaccine initiation among 11- and 12-year-olds by 5.4 percentage points more than control clinics after six months. Training in open-ended “conversations” did not raise it beyond the existing trend (Brewer et al., 2017).

What this means for Kazakhstan. The three studies agree that the messenger matters as much as the message: Denmark recovered through doctors and other parents, not through the state speaking alone, and doctors move parents most when they speak with confidence. Access is not Kazakhstan’s main barrier, since the vaccine is already free (Ministry of Healthcare, 2026) and organised around a school consent form. What is missing is a fast answer to the rumour, aimed at hesitant parents and spoken by voices they trust.

The gap

The research shows whom Kazakh parents trust and what works abroad, but not how to bring a trusted voice to parents at the moment they decide.

Read the full analysis

Two bodies of research sit side by side but do not meet. The Kazakh studies explain why mothers hesitate: they know little, fear infertility, and trust doctors far more than the government (Babi et al., 2023; Aimagambetova et al., 2022; Kassabekova et al., 2025). The international studies explain what turns hesitation around: early messages, fast answers to rumours, and confident, trusted messengers such as doctors and other parents (Gallagher et al., 2017; Hansen et al., 2020; Brewer et al., 2017).

The link between them is missing. All three Kazakh studies were carried out in cities before the 2024 relaunch, and none asks what parents need at the moment of the school consent form. Gallagher’s advice, meanwhile, leans on a government voice, the one Kazakh parents trust least. No study reviewed here asks how Kazakhstan can bring a trusted medical voice to hesitant parents at the moment they decide, for example in a parents’ chat at 21:47. That is the gap this project addresses.

Section 4The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

The proposal: «Саулық» (Saulyq): health advice for parents

Saulyq is a doctor-checked HPV chatbot for parents, offered with the school consent form, that answers only from official sources and tells parents when to see a doctor or call the 7701 hotline.

See the full design

Saulyq (Kazakh for "health, well-being") is a small chatbot for parents, available in Kazakh and Russian for the two weeks around each school's HPV consent deadline. Its design builds on the three documented cases below (two successes and one counter-case) and on the Kazakh evidence about trust and knowledge:

  • Where it lives: a link and QR code printed on the consent form, re-sent by the class teacher to the parents' chat. The Chinese trial reached parents through class teachers and parents' messenger groups and re-sent its link every four days.
  • What it knows: nothing but vetted content: the National Center for Public Health's egu.kz pages and Ministry guidance, reviewed by gynaecologists. It uses retrieval-augmented generation, meaning the model answers only from that library and says "I don't know: ask your polyclinic doctor or call 7701" when the answer is not there.
  • Whose voice: answers are visibly signed by named doctors, not by "the Ministry", because mothers trust medical workers several times more than the government (Figure 5). The tone is plain and warm: in the Chinese trial, parents who used the "nurse" persona were 2.08 times more likely to start vaccination than those using only the "expert" persona ("nurse" there describes a tone, not who checks the answers).
  • What it never does: it asks for no name, phone number or school, keeps no link to the refusal register, and is labelled as AI, as Kazakhstan's AI Law requires.
  • Who runs it: the National Center for Public Health, with a small team of medical reviewers.

Three documented cases elsewhere

Case 1 · China · Success

An HPV vaccine chatbot for parents of schoolgirls

Source: Hou et al. (2025), Nature Medicine: a cluster randomised trial in China, January–May 2024.

What was built. Researchers built a chatbot on GPT-4 that answers only from a knowledge base drawn from China’s health authorities and checked by public health experts, with a formal “expert” and a warm “nurse” persona. The trial covered 180 middle-school classes; parents in 90 of them could use the chatbot for two weeks, and the link was re-sent every four days.

What it achieved. 7.1% of parents in the chatbot group booked or got the vaccine for their daughter within two weeks, against 1.8% with usual promotion. About half (49.1%) consulted a health professional about HPV vaccination, against 17.6%. In rural areas, parents with the chatbot were 8.81 times as likely to book or vaccinate.

What Saulyq takes from it (my design choice, not the paper’s): a verified library only, a warm tone, links sent through class teachers and parent chats, and a route to health workers.

Case 2 · Hong Kong · Success

A rule-bound “hybrid” chatbot for older adults

Source: Wang et al. (2025), JAMA Network Open: a randomised clinical trial in Hong Kong.

What was built. A chatbot for adults aged 65+ who use WhatsApp. Fixed, rule-based messages matched each person’s readiness to vaccinate, and an AI question-and-answer part replied to their questions in real time. It ran in four monthly sessions.

What it achieved. In a randomised trial of 374 people, 29.4% of the hybrid-chatbot group got the pneumococcal vaccine, against 18.7% of those who only received a link to a standard video. More of them engaged at least once: 79.7% against 57.8%.

What Saulyq takes from it (my design choice, not the paper’s): strict rules about what the bot may say, combined with real answers to people’s own questions, on a messenger they already use.

Counter-case · US, UK, Canada · No added benefit

A one-off AI chat against government health materials

Source: Sehgal et al. (2026), JAMA Network Open: a randomised clinical trial in the US, UK and Canada.

What was built. A GPT-4o chatbot prompted to encourage HPV vaccination, tested in one conversation of at least three minutes with 1,297 parents recruited online, and compared with government public health materials and with no message.

What it achieved. Right after the chat, intention to vaccinate rose, but not more than with the government materials (effect sizes 0.48 and 0.33 for two chatbot styles, against 0.53). After 45 days neither chatbot still raised intention, while the materials kept a modest effect. No group reported vaccinating more.

What Saulyq takes from it (my design choice, not the paper’s): a short persuasive chat is not enough. Saulyq informs rather than persuades, stays available through the consent window, and must be tested against an improved egu.kz page, not against nothing.

Limits of the evidence. Hou compared the chatbot with usual local promotion, not with good written materials, and in China parents mostly pay for the vaccine themselves. Wang studied older adults rather than parents and a different vaccine, although, as with HPV in Kazakhstan, the vaccine was free. Sehgal tested a single short chat with online volunteers. Together they show that grounded, repeated, human-linked chatbots can raise vaccination, not that Saulyq would.

EnvironmentalLOW
Saulyq does not train a new AI model. It runs an existing one, plus a search in a short library, and only for about two weeks a year around each school’s consent deadline. So the computing power it needs, and the energy that uses, is small compared with an always-on chatbot. Data centres also use water, both to cool servers and to produce their electricity (Li et al., 2023). Saulyq would run on servers in Kazakhstan, where coal produces over 70% of electricity (IEA, 2022). The cost is small, not zero. Most AI providers do not disclose their energy or water use (Luccioni et al., 2024), so I give no figure per question.
EconomicMEDIUM
What it costs: building the bot, hosting it in Kazakhstan, writing the content in Kazakh and Russian, and paying the doctors who check and update it. Who pays: the state. Keeping data in the country rules out a cheaper foreign AI service; a model hosted in Kazakhstan is easier to control, but it may write Kazakh less well, and that must be tested. Who profits: the company that builds and hosts the bot and, if more girls are vaccinated, the maker of Gardasil, the US-made vaccine used (Kazinform, 2025). Saulyq should be open about this if parents ask. The real test is value for money: does Saulyq get more girls vaccinated, per tenge spent, than simply improving the egu.kz website?
LabourMEDIUM
No job disappears, but work shifts. Polyclinic doctors and 7701 operators take more appointments and calls from parents the bot sends their way, and class teachers send the link to parents’ chats, usually as unpaid extra work. Saulyq also depends on people that parents never see. Doctors check the library before launch, acting as what Tubaro et al. (2020) call “verifiers”. They do not read each live answer, so the library must be right. Translators prepare every entry in Kazakh and Russian. Someone must keep the library up to date: when the programme was extended to girls aged 14–17 in May 2026, every related answer would have had to change. And the AI model itself was built with the work of data workers who labelled text, work that usually stays invisible (Williams & Miceli, 2023).
Political & governanceHIGH
Who controls it: the National Center for Public Health would run Saulyq and decide what goes into its library; whoever owns the AI model controls how it writes. Servers and data stay in Kazakhstan. If the government runs the bot, parents who distrust the government may distrust the bot too. Kazakhstan’s AI Law bans manipulative AI, so Saulyq must give information, not pressure parents. Government guidelines require human oversight of AI, and research on AI in Kazakhstan’s public sector warns that the main risks are centralised data and weak accountability (Buribayeva et al., 2026). The worst misuse would be to start saving chats and match them with the refusal register to find “hesitant” families.
Data ethicsHIGH
What data: only the question a parent types. Saulyq asks for no name and saves nothing. Whose consent: the parent chooses to use it and must be told clearly that they are talking to an AI. The daughter, whose health is being discussed, is never asked. What could go wrong: the AI ignores its library and invents an answer, which is why every answer must show its source; an outdated entry gives every parent the same wrong answer at once; a fake “Saulyq” link appears in a parents’ chat, just as the infertility rumour did; or whoever runs Saulyq later decides to start saving questions, so “no saved questions” must be written into its rules, not only its design.

VerdictShould Kazakhstan do this?

Not as a national rollout. Yes, as a measured pilot, only under these conditions:

  1. Inform, don't persuade. Answers come only from vetted egu.kz content, are signed by doctors, and the bot says "I don't know" rather than guessing.
  2. Protect the parent. No identification, no link to the refusal register, storage in Kazakhstan, clear AI labelling.
  3. Send people to humans. Every conversation ends with a path to a polyclinic doctor or the 7701 hotline, and those doctors get the same library and the time to use it.
  4. Prove it beats a leaflet. Pilot it in the regions with the most refusals, Aktobe and Zhambyl, against an improved egu.kz FAQ, and measure signed consent forms, not opinions. If it does not beat the FAQ, do not scale it.

The evidence says the problem is less a lack of facts than a lack of a trusted voice at the right moment. AI can deliver the right facts at 21:47. It cannot, by itself, become the trusted voice. That has to come from the doctors behind it.
