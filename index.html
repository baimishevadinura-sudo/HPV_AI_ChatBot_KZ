Section 1The problem: a free vaccine, a hesitant parent

Cervical cancer is preventable, yet Kazakhstan’s first HPV vaccine programme collapsed under parental refusal. The vaccine is now back.

Read the full analysis

Cervical cancer is one of the most preventable cancers, yet it is the second most common cancer among women in Kazakhstan (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). In 2021 it had a crude incidence of 18.3 cases and a crude mortality of 9.1 deaths per 100,000 women, which means roughly 1,800 new cases and 600 deaths every year (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). Almost all of it is caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV), and a vaccine given before adolescence prevents most of it. The World Health Organization's goal is for 90% of girls to be fully vaccinated by age 15 by 2030 (WHO, 2020).

Kazakhstan has tried this before. Its first HPV vaccination pilot collapsed after negative media reports about side effects were followed by mass parental refusal (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). The sources do not even agree on its dates (see the timeline). A similar story played out in Japan, where reports of side effects cut HPV coverage to below 1% (Babi et al., 2023). Halting the programme also cost Kazakhstan its place in the global vaccine supply queue (Astana Times, 2025).

Since 2024 the vaccine is free again, but each year first doses still reach less than half of the target group.

Read the full analysis

Since September 2024 the vaccine has been free again, delivered through schools to girls aged 11–13 and, since May 2026, to girls aged 14–17 (Ministry of Healthcare, 2026). Figure 1 shows how far the programme has come and how far it still has to go.

First-dose coverage rose from 38% to 47% in the programme's first full year, but the number of first doses given in 2025 still equalled less than half of the target group. Low HPV coverage is common worldwide: WHO estimates global first-dose coverage among girls at 33% in 2025 (WHO, 2026). In Kazakhstan, though, it sits next to excellent coverage for routine childhood vaccines: in 2022, 97.1% of infants received their primary DTP series (Kassabekova et al., 2025). The problem is specific to this vaccine.

Who is affected, and who decides

Girls are at risk, but parents, usually mothers, decide by signing a voluntary consent form.

Read the full analysis

The people at risk are girls, but the people who decide are their parents. Vaccination is voluntary and requires a parent's signature (Ministry of Healthcare, 2026), and in practice the decision-maker is usually the mother: mothers made up 87.7% of participating parents in a large Chinese school trial (Hou et al., 2025), and Kazakh survey research focuses on mothers for the same reason (Babi et al., 2023). So the unit of this problem is a single household decision, made quickly, often in a messenger chat, about a vaccine linked to sex and cancer and to a programme that failed once before.

Kazakhstan gave up on this vaccine once, after negative media reports turned into mass parental refusal. Whether the relaunch ends differently depends on one question: at the moment a parent decides, whom do they trust, and can a chatbot carry that trust when the state cannot?

Three forces stand in the way: distrust of the state, an information gap and stigma around sexual health.

Read the full analysis

The story has three acts: a rumour, a retreat and a sequel. The first programme was not stopped because the vaccine was shown to be unsafe: reports of perceived side effects were followed by mass parental refusal, and the state withdrew (Kassymbekova et al., 2023). The relaunch now faces a new generation of parents, the same messenger chats and the memory of that retreat. The evidence reviewed below points to three forces. The first is institutional distrust: Kazakh women trust doctors and scientists far more than the government or the pharmaceutical industry, and women affected by the failed pilot are less positive about the vaccine (Aimagambetova et al., 2022). The second is an information gap: most mothers know very little about HPV, and the ones who know least are undecided rather than hostile (Babi et al., 2023). The third is stigma: mothers who find it hard to talk about sexual health with their daughters are much more likely to oppose the vaccine. The first time, negative media reports were enough to tip parents into refusal; surveys since then show all three forces are still present. Whether the relaunch repeats the old story depends on who parents hear from at the moment they decide. Any tool the state builds speaks with the state's voice, the voice parents trust least. Whether an AI chatbot can instead carry the voice of doctors, before the rumour wins again, is a question about trust, memory and power, not only about medicine, and that is what makes it a question for political science.

Section 2Background & data: what is counted, and what is not

Four kinds of data exist: official coverage reports, Ministry totals, a refusal register and surveys. They do not fully agree.

Read the full analysis

Four kinds of data describe HPV vaccination in Kazakhstan. Administrative coverage is reported every year by the Ministry to WHO and UNICEF, which publish their own estimates alongside it. Running totals appear in Ministry press posts. A national refusal register records parents who formally decline a vaccine, and started recording HPV in 2025 (Chayakova et al., 2026, preprint). Finally, a handful of surveys and focus groups describe what mothers and health workers know and believe. Each source answers a different question, and the gaps between them matter as much as the numbers.

Same doses, two different percentages

The same 90,613 doses give 47% or 43.91%, depending on who counts the target group.

Read the full analysis

The clearest example comes from the official coverage data. For 2025, Kazakhstan's own report and the WHO/UNICEF estimate count exactly the same 90,613 first doses. But they divide them by different target populations, and so they arrive at different coverage rates.

A three-point gap may look minor, but it means that nobody can say with certainty how many girls the programme is supposed to reach. Every target, budget and evaluation, including one for an AI tool, rests on that denominator.

Four Kazakh studies ask, in different ways, what people think about the HPV vaccine. They use different methods and different samples, and they do not produce a single answer.

The comparison is revealing. Two surveys in the same cities, in the same months, find 54% and 45% positive, and the figure drops to 41% among the people who actually decide: mothers of daughters. Part of the gap is likely about whom they sampled: the first survey also included women without children. In these data, the more personally the decision concerns people, the less positive they appear. The focus groups explain the numbers rather than repeat them: they show that hesitation is built on specific beliefs (infertility, heredity) and on memories of the failed pilot. Health workers, asked about their patients, name the same two barriers first: mistrust and fear of side effects. All four studies point the same way, but none of them measures attitudes after the 2024 relaunch, and none of the parent studies is representative of rural families.

A register that sometimes counts backwards

In its first year recording HPV, the register counted 9,209 refusals, almost two-thirds in Aktobe and Zhambyl. The register itself has errors.

Read the full analysis

The refusal register should be the most direct measure of hesitancy. According to a new preprint that has not yet been peer-reviewed, registered refusals of all vaccines rose from 39,792 to 66,240 between 2024 and 2026. In its first year recording HPV, the register counted 9,209 HPV refusals, 13.9% of all refusers, with almost two-thirds in Aktobe (3,037) and Zhambyl (2,791) (Chayakova et al., 2026, preprint). The same study, however, finds that the register records 3.35–3.84 times more BCG refusals than routine coverage reports do, and that in 46 of 180 region–vaccine pairs a cumulative count went down from one year to the next, which a cumulative count should never do. Across all vaccines, personal beliefs were the leading reason (62.1%), followed by religion (15.5%). The register does not publish reasons for refusing HPV separately; for those, the focus groups with mothers of girls are the main source (Kassabekova et al., 2025).

Taken together, the data is good enough to say that coverage is far below target and that refusal is concentrated geographically. It is not good enough to say precisely how many girls are missed, who their parents are, or what would change their minds. Any honest proposal has to be designed for that uncertainty, and has to measure its own effect rather than assume it.

Section 3Current state of research

Three approaches: what mothers believe, what health workers know, and which interventions work.

Read the full analysis

Research on HPV vaccination in Kazakhstan is recent and small, and several of the key studies come from one research group at Nazarbayev University. It falls into three approaches: surveys of what mothers know and believe, qualitative and survey studies of the health workers who deliver the vaccine, and international trials of interventions, including the first trials of AI chatbots.

1. What mothers know and believe

Mothers know little, the undecided know least, and they trust doctors far more than the state.

Read the full analysis

Two surveys conducted between December 2021 and February 2022 in four large cities give the clearest picture. Among 191 mothers, only 45% had a positive attitude to HPV vaccination; among mothers of daughters, the group that actually decides, the figure was 41% (Babi et al., 2023). Knowledge was strikingly low, a mean of 2.4 correct answers out of 12, and the most important finding is who knew least: mothers with neutral attitudes, whose median score was zero.

Where do mothers get their information? Almost 40% from social media and TV, and 35% from gynaecologists and family doctors; schools and paediatricians were among the least-used sources, and of mothers who reached a decision, only 36% found it easy (Babi et al., 2023). That matters because the programme itself is delivered through schools. Stigma also appears directly in the data: among mothers who agreed it was hard to talk to their daughters about sexual health, 46% had negative attitudes to the vaccine.

The second survey, of 233 women, found 54% positive, and asked a question that goes to the heart of this project: whom do they trust?

Women trust scientists and medical workers several times more than the government, and the least trusted source is the messenger apps where rumours spread. Lower education, more children, belief in alternative medicine and having been affected by the failed 2013 programme were all linked to less positive attitudes (Aimagambetova et al., 2022). Focus groups in five cities add the human detail: mothers feared infertility, thought cervical cancer was hereditary, preferred gynaecologists and oncologists as messengers, and disagreed by region about whether schools are safe places to vaccinate (Kassabekova et al., 2025).

3. What works: from leaflets to chatbots

Reviews conclude that education alone is not enough. A chatbot raised vaccination in one trial and added nothing in another.

Read the full analysis

Internationally, the evidence on how to raise HPV vaccination is large but lopsided. A systematic review of 79 intervention studies found that 72.2% were conducted in the United States, the largest group (31.6%) were purely informational, and 76.3% worked at a single level (only parents, or only clinics); the authors conclude that education alone is not enough and that combined, multi-level programmes are needed (Escoffery et al., 2023). A newer line of research tests large-language-model chatbots. Two randomised trials of HPV chatbots for parents reach opposite conclusions: one in Chinese schools found that a chatbot raised actual vaccination (Hou et al., 2025), while one with online parent panels in three Western countries found it no better than a government leaflet (Sehgal et al., 2026).

Why the two trials disagree

Read together, the trials suggest that a chatbot is not magic persuasion. Hou's bot worked when it was embedded in a real school process, available for days rather than minutes, grounded in verified content, and when it sent parents to health workers: about half of the parents in the chatbot group consulted one. Sehgal's bot, a single persuasive chat against a well-designed leaflet, added nothing. The two trials even disagree on tone: Hou's warm nurse persona did better, while Sehgal's exploratory analysis linked empathy-heavy conversations to lower intention. Kazakhstan's situation sits closer to Hou's: a school programme and messenger-based parent communities. But one difference matters: the vaccine is free in Kazakhstan and was paid out of pocket in Hou's study areas, so the barriers differ and the size of the effect may not transfer. So any Kazakh tool should be tested against an improved egu.kz page, not against nothing.

The gap

None of the studies reviewed here tests a digital tool in Kazakhstan, or asks parents what they need at the moment of consent.

Read the full analysis

Three things are missing. None of the studies reviewed here tests a digital or AI intervention for vaccination in Kazakhstan or Central Asia. None asks parents what they need to know at the moment of the school consent form, after the 2024 relaunch. And none connects the two findings that sit side by side in this literature: that the undecided middle knows least, and that the people they trust most, doctors, are not the ones present when the rumour arrives at 21:47. This project addresses that gap.

Section 4The AI-assisted solution, and what it would cost

The proposal: «Саулық» (Saulyq): health advice for parents

Saulyq is a doctor-checked HPV chatbot for parents, offered with the school consent form, that answers only from official sources and hands over to a nurse.

See the full design

Saulyq (Kazakh for "health, well-being") is a small chatbot for parents, available in Kazakh and Russian for the two weeks around each school's HPV consent deadline. Its design builds on the two documented case studies reviewed in Section 3, the school-based chatbot trial in China (Hou et al., 2025) and the online trial in the US, Canada and UK (Sehgal et al., 2026), and on the Kazakh evidence about trust and knowledge:

  • Where it lives: a link and QR code printed on the consent form, re-sent by the class teacher to the parents' chat. The Chinese trial reached parents through class teachers and parents' messenger groups and re-sent its link every four days.
  • What it knows: nothing but vetted content: the National Center for Public Health's egu.kz pages and Ministry guidance, reviewed by gynaecologists. It uses retrieval-augmented generation, meaning the model answers only from that library and says "I don't know: ask your school nurse or call 7701" when the answer is not there.
  • Whose voice: answers are visibly signed by named doctors, not by "the Ministry", because mothers trust medical workers several times more than the government (Figure 5). The tone is plain and warm: in the Chinese trial, parents who used the "nurse" persona were 2.08 times more likely to start vaccination than those using only the "expert" persona.
  • What it never does: it asks for no name, phone number or school, keeps no link to the refusal register, and is labelled as AI, as Kazakhstan's AI Law requires.
  • Who runs it: the National Center for Public Health, with a small team of medical reviewers. A second version of the same library gives school nurses a quick-reference tool, since nurses had the lowest HPV knowledge.

VerdictShould Kazakhstan do this?

Draft verdict for you to accept, change or reject. The verdict is the part of the project most clearly yours: rewrite it in your own words.

Not as a national rollout. Yes, as a measured pilot, only under these conditions:

  1. Inform, don't persuade. Answers come only from vetted egu.kz content, are signed by doctors, and the bot says "I don't know" rather than guessing.
  2. Protect the parent. No identification, no link to the refusal register, storage in Kazakhstan, clear AI labelling.
  3. Send people to humans. Every conversation ends with a path to a school nurse or polyclinic, and those nurses get the same library and the time to use it.
  4. Prove it beats a leaflet. Pilot it in the regions with the most refusals, Aktobe and Zhambyl, against an improved egu.kz FAQ, and measure signed consent forms, not opinions. If it does not beat the FAQ, do not scale it.

The evidence says the problem is less a lack of facts than a lack of a trusted voice at the right moment. AI can deliver the right facts at 21:47. It cannot, by itself, become the trusted voice. That has to come from the doctors behind it.
